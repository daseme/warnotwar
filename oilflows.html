1 · The physical break

Hormuz tanker activity, 7-day average of PortWatch tanker counts · index: 100 = its normal late-2025 level, 5 = 5% of that

This is a tanker-activity index, not barrels of crude. It answers when shipping activity broke, day by day.

2 · Claimed vs. observed flow

U.S. statements vs independent estimates (trackers, banks, market participants) · mb/d = million barrels per day · dated events, not a daily line

Wartime tanker tracking is unusually uncertain because vessels may switch off their transponders. U.S. military surveillance can therefore see ships that commercial tracking misses. We show both the U.S. government's stated oil flow and the volume independently estimated by commercial trackers. The difference is labeled unverified until subsequent loading, arrival and official trade data can resolve it.

Part of the confusion is that several different official numbers are circulating, and they answer different questions:

Ships operating without AIS (the position beacon ships broadcast) may explain part of the gap — a missed supertanker is roughly 2 million barrels. But oil does not disappear with a transponder signal: every unverified million barrels a day is seven million barrels a week that must load, sail, unload, and eventually appear in import and trade statistics. This page labels the difference unverified; it does not decide who is right. Comparisons never mix scopes or units, and the PortWatch index above is a third, different measure — publicly visible AIS activity — never converted into barrels. 3 · Official confirmation

Iraq Basrah + Kuwait official/calibrated crude exports · 7-day average, million barrels/day

The "real barrels" companion: official monthly totals from SOMO (Iraq) and JODI (Kuwait), allocated to days using PortWatch loading activity where available. Monthly books are slower and smoother than the traffic sensor — that is why both layers exist. Saudi national exports are kept separate because they include Red Sea bypass routes and are not a Hormuz-only measure.

5 · The buffer

Stored U.S. crude, in days of refinery use · weekly since 1982 · EIA

Stored U.S. crude, including the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, divided by that week's refinery crude inputs. This is a cushion ratio, not a countdown — domestic production and imports continue, refinery runs change, and SPR releases are policy decisions. The SPR share is also rate-limited: it can only be drawn down so fast, so the cushion is not one uniform pool. The denominator is seasonal: days of supply can fall simply because refineries run harder.

6 · The market response

BNO, an oil fund ordinary investors can buy, in dollars · tracks Brent imperfectly — not the actual barrel price

Prices repriced sharply while the physical shutdown was developing and peaked only after tanker activity had already collapsed. Hover a chart: values are marked as an actual market observation or a carried-forward last close.

8 · How we know

The traffic sensor. IMF PortWatch derives daily tanker-activity signals from AIS data — the position beacons ships broadcast — at the Strait of Hormuz and at export terminals. It is fast and shows when activity disappears, but it does not count barrels of crude on each ship.

The official tally. SOMO (Iraq) and JODI (Kuwait, Saudi Arabia) publish official monthly export figures. They arrive with a lag and cannot pinpoint the day a flow stopped, but they anchor the story in real reported volumes. Where the within-month timing is unknown, the daily line is deliberately flat at the official monthly level.

The detour counters. Saudi Arabia can ship west from Yanbu on the Red Sea; UAE flows can move toward Fujairah outside the Strait. Watching Gulf-side and bypass terminals separately shows whether activity is rerouted rather than simply gone.

The buffer gauge. The U.S. holds crude in commercial inventories and in the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Dividing those stocks (EIA, weekly) by what U.S. refineries are currently processing gives the size of the cushion in "days of refinery use." It is a ratio, not a countdown — production, imports, and policy all keep moving.

The market price. Brent spot and the BNO ETF show how traders priced the disruption as it developed.

The claims ledger. Officials describe the flow; independent trackers estimate it; the two disagree. Every recorded statement is written down in a standard form with its source, compared only with figures that count the same thing, and the difference is labeled unverified — no one is declared right — until loading, arrival and official trade data can resolve it.

Iran and Qatar are intentionally excluded: public terminal coverage is too sparse or incomplete for a defensible daily series. Their absence means "not measurable here," not zero exports.
