The Salt Caverns

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve is sixty holes dissolved in four salt domes under Texas and Louisiana. This page shows how they hold oil, how the oil comes out, and what changes as the level falls.

1 · the basics

Why there is a reserve at all

In October 1973 the Arab oil states stopped selling to the United States over its support for Israel in the Yom Kippur War. The embargo lasted five months. The price of imported crude roughly quadrupled, filling stations ran dry and drivers queued for hours. The country was importing about a third of its oil and had nothing set aside.

before it

The cushion used to be Texas. For forty years the Texas Railroad Commission held wells back from full production to keep prices up, which left spare capacity that could be opened in an emergency. In March 1972 it let every well run flat out. The spare capacity was gone a year before the embargo.

The only federal stockpile was the Naval Petroleum Reserves: oilfields at Elk Hills in California and Teapot Dome in Wyoming, set aside in 1912 and 1915 so the Navy would have fuel for its ships. The oil stayed in the ground. Elk Hills was sold in 1998.

who decided

Richard Nixon answered the embargo with Project Independence in November 1973, a promise to end oil imports by 1980. Henry Kissinger pushed the allies to act together, and in November 1974 the United States and fifteen other countries founded the International Energy Agency. Each promised to hold ninety days of imports and to share oil in a crisis.

Congress wrote the reserve into the Energy Policy and Conservation Act. Gerald Ford signed it on December 22, 1975. It allowed up to a billion barrels; the working goal became 750 million. The first oil went underground at West Hackberry in July 1977.

where the oil comes from

The government buys crude on the open market by competitive bid and takes delivery by pipeline or tanker at the four sites. The first barrels were Saudi light. Much of what sits in the caverns today is Mexican Isthmus, North Sea Brent and Forties, and Mars from the Gulf of Mexico. Bayou Choctaw still holds Iranian Light bought before the 1979 revolution.

From 1999 the reserve also filled with royalty oil: companies drilling in federal waters paid the government in barrels instead of dollars, about 140 million of them. Loans add more, because a refiner that borrows in a crisis pays back later with extra barrels. Across the whole reserve the average price paid is $29.70 a barrel.

how it is used

The oil is crude. Nobody burns crude; refineries turn it into gasoline, diesel and jet fuel. A release is either a sale by online auction or a loan. Refiners and traders take delivery at the sites into the same pipelines and tankers that carry everyone else’s oil. Since 2015 the barrels can also be exported.

The reserve has never held gasoline or diesel. A separate one million barrels of heating oil sits in tanks in the Northeast. What a release changes is the price: more crude on offer means a lower price, here and abroad, because oil is one world market.

2 · where it is

Four salt domes, sixty caverns

Gulf Coast salt domes are fingers of Jurassic salt pushed up through miles of sediment. Salt does not let oil through, and it flows slowly enough to heal its own cracks. It is the cheapest place on earth to keep crude: about $3.50 a barrel to build, against $15 to $18 for a steel tank.

Each small cylinder is one cavern. Oil is dark, brine is blue. Per-site inventories are DOE’s figures for ; they do not update weekly. The reserve is drawn cavern by cavern, so the picture shows full caverns and empty ones rather than every cavern half full.

3 · down in the salt

Half a mile down, a hole the size of a skyscraper

A cavern the Department of Energy built is a cylinder about 200 feet across and 2,000 feet tall. Its roof is around 2,500 feet down and its floor around 4,500. It holds ten to twelve million barrels. Chicago’s Willis Tower would fit inside with room to spare.

Nobody dug them. A well is drilled into the salt and river water is pumped down it. The water dissolves salt and comes back up as brine, which is piped to the Gulf or injected into deep wells. Seven barrels of water make one barrel of space. A cavern took two and a half to three and a half years to leach.

The caverns sit 750 feet apart, centre to centre, leaving about 550 feet of solid salt between them. That web of salt is what holds everything up, and it thins a little every time a cavern is emptied.

Eleven of the sixty caverns were not built for the reserve. They were leached for brine in the 1930s to 1960s and bought in 1977. They are shallower, wider and irregular, and most can be emptied only once more before they are retired.

The salt is warm. Oil arrives at 70 to 80 degrees and warms to 120 to 130 over the years. The heat difference between roof and floor keeps the oil slowly turning over, so it never separates.
4 · how the oil moves

Water goes in at the bottom. Oil comes out at the top.

A cavern is always full of liquid. Oil floats on brine. To take oil out, river water is pumped down a hanging pipe to the floor. The water pushes the oil up the space around the pipe and out the wellhead. To put oil in, the flow reverses: oil goes down onto the roof and brine comes up the pipe to a pond.

One well moves 60,000 to 90,000 barrels a day, so a ten million barrel cavern takes about four months to empty. The water that goes in is not saturated, so it keeps dissolving salt: every hundred barrels of oil out costs fifteen barrels of rock wall. A full drawdown grows the cavern by about fifteen percent. Each cavern was designed for five.

Refilling is slower. The brine pushed out has to go somewhere, and the disposal wells and Gulf pipelines set the pace. The whole reserve was designed to take in 785,000 barrels a day and give out 4.4 million. Today it can take in about 440,000.

The oil never quite runs out. About two percent stays behind on purpose: a blanket of oil on the roof so that water never touches the ceiling. Across the reserve that is about twelve million barrels that will never come up.

5 · when it runs low

Emptiness is not the danger. Speed is.

A cavern is never empty. Water replaces oil barrel for barrel, and the pressure stays where it was. What a low reserve loses is the ability to deliver fast.

Each cavern flows through its own well at a set rate. The design figure of 4.4 million barrels a day assumes all sixty caverns hold oil and all sixty wells can run. Oil in fewer caverns means fewer wells.

The reserve is drawn cavern by cavern rather than a little from each, so this bites early. West Hackberry held 30 million barrels in August 2026, about three caverns’ worth out of twenty-one. GAO had already rated it at 58 percent of its design rate in December, when it held 88.

The chart models this: the rate each site can deliver, times the share of its caverns that still hold oil above the roof blanket. The solid line has all four sites running. The dashed line leaves Big Hill closed, as it has been since January 2025. GAO’s measured 2.7 million barrels a day sits above both lines: a well can run a little faster than its even share, and GAO rated Bryan Mound at its full 1.5 with fourteen of nineteen caverns holding oil. The model errs on the cautious side.

People have drawn lines on that tank. They mean different things.

300. Amos Hochstein, who ran energy policy in the last White House: “I don’t know anyone who believes we can go below 300.” The reserve went below 300 in the week ending August 7, 2026.

252.4. Federal law. Below this Congress forbids limited drawdowns, the small non-emergency releases capped at 30 million barrels. A presidential emergency finding is not bound by it. The 2026 release is an emergency exchange, so the line does not stop it.

170. Rapidan Energy’s “soft-ish floor,” where cavern integrity and pumping limits argue against going further.

70. The Department of Energy’s stated physical minimum. Roughly the roof blankets plus the heels. DOE’s spokesman: “The caverns are always full. All that changes is the ratio of oil and water that is filling them.”

243. Where analysts expect the level to settle if the full 172 million barrels of the 2026 release are delivered.

6 · wear

Five drawdowns per cavern

Every full drawdown dissolves salt equal to fifteen percent of the cavern’s volume. The cavern grows; the salt between it and its neighbour shrinks. Sandia National Laboratories counts a fifteen percent growth as one drawdown spent, and no cavern gets more than five.

At the end of 2024, 47 of the 60 caverns had at least four drawdowns left. One had one. At Bayou Choctaw three caverns are on their last, and two may already be connected underground.

Partial drawdowns wear caverns faster than full ones. Water enters at the hanging pipe near the floor, so a partial draw leaches only the bottom, and repeating it carves the same spot again and again. The reserve made fourteen releases smaller than ten million barrels between 1996 and 2014.

Salt also closes in on its own. Under the weight of the rock above, the walls creep inward and the reserve loses about 1.2 million barrels of space a year, plus another million from well repairs that take a cavern off pressure for months. The caverns were built for a 25-year life. The second-newest well is 38 years old.

Wells worry Sandia more than caverns now. At Big Hill more than 70 percent of the wells have moderate to severe casing deformation where the moving salt meets the rigid caprock. In May 2024 a Bryan Mound well failed with no warning and about 400,000 barrels of oil were lost. Most West Hackberry caverns have a single well; if it fails, the oil is unreachable until a second is drilled.

7 · speed

Thirteen days to the first barrel

The reserve’s own performance rule: after a presidential order, run a competitive sale, award contracts and have oil flowing within thirteen days, at full rate by day fifteen. In March 2026 the first shipments left nine days after the order. In 1991 it took twenty.

From a full reserve the design lets 4.4 million barrels a day flow for ninety days, about 400 million barrels. Then sites start to run dry and the rate steps down: 3.8, then 3.4, then 1.9 by day 180. The smaller sites go first. Bayou Choctaw holds 76 million barrels and flows at half a million a day, so it lasts about five months at full tilt.

No release has come close. The 2022 release ran at one million barrels a day for six months, about a fifth of the design rate. The 2026 release averaged 800,000 a day and peaked at 1.4 million in one week in May. Both were set by what buyers would take and by contract terms, not by the pumps. In 2026 the constraint was also Big Hill: closed, with 89 million barrels sitting untouched in it.

The barrels have to fit into pipelines that are already full of commercial oil. DOE said in 2016 that the reserve “can no longer bring oil to market without disturbing commercial oil flows,” and that in a Hormuz closure the United States could not meet its share of an allied release without new marine terminals. The terminals were never built.

8 · run the reserve

You have the keys. The strait is shut.

Each turn is one week. Set the release rate. The caverns can only give what their wells can flow, and the number of wells falls as caverns empty. The shortfall lasts as long as it lasts; you will not be told. Hurricanes, well failures and refiners who will not take sour crude happen when they happen.

9 · the record

Fifty years of filling and draining

First oil went into West Hackberry on July 21, 1977: 412,000 barrels of Saudi light. The peak was 726.6 million barrels on December 27, 2009. Since 2015 Congress has sold oil to pay for other things, and since 2022 two presidents have released more than 300 million barrels.

emergency releases

1991 Desert Storm, 17.3 million barrels sold. 2005 Katrina, 11 million sold and 9.8 loaned. 2011 Libya, 30.6 million. 2022 Ukraine, 180 million at about $95 a barrel. 2026 Hormuz, 172 million authorised as loans, to be repaid with a premium of 18 to 28 percent from November 2026.

what it cost

The oil in the reserve was bought at an average of $29.70 a barrel. Running the four sites costs about $206 million a year with 120 federal staff and roughly 630 contractors. Life Extension 2, the $1.4 billion rebuild of the surface plant, was due in 2024 and is now expected in 2028 with West Hackberry dropped from it.

what it covers

The United States uses about 20 million barrels of petroleum a day. A full reserve is 35 days of that; today’s is 14. Against the 20 million barrels a day that used to pass through Hormuz, even the design rate of 4.4 replaces about a fifth. The reserve moves the world price, which is the price Americans pay; it does not replace the world’s oil.

Where the numbers come from

The live number. EIA weekly series WCSSTUS1, refreshed every Wednesday evening by a GitHub Action into spr/data/spr_stocks.json. Monthly series MCSSTUS1 fills in 1977 to 1982.

The model in section 5 and the game. Each site’s rate is its design drawdown rate times the share of its caverns that still hold oil above a two percent heel, times an availability flag (Big Hill zero). Oil is assumed to sit in as few caverns as possible. Leaching is 15 percent of oil displaced. Fill is capped at GAO’s 0.44 million barrels a day. Crisis length in the game is random within a range. These are teaching simplifications; DOE has published no drawdown table below 430 million barrels.

The way out, in the game. Each dome’s flow is the smallest of three links: its pumps, its line to the pipeline system, and the buyers and docks at the end. The totals are DOE’s: three pipeline systems (Seaway from Bryan Mound, Texoma from Big Hill and West Hackberry, Capline from Bayou Choctaw), 2.22 million barrels a day of contracted marine capacity at commercial terminals, 0.4 at the St. James terminal the reserve owns, and a 4.4 design rate when every link is built. The split of those totals between the three systems, the refiners’ appetite on each, and the halving of shared lines and contracted docks by commercial oil after 2012 are this model’s, sized so the links add up to the design rate. The 2016 review’s finding that the reserve “can no longer bring oil to market without disturbing commercial oil flows” is the reason the halving exists. The terminal you can build in the game is the one that review asked for and never got.
