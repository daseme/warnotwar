The current moment.

Six figures from Uppsala's conflict files, each set against every year the files cover, back to 1946 for the counts and 1989 for the deaths. The line at the top says how many of them are the highest yet.

    What 1994 is

    1994 is Rwanda. Of the 824,177 deaths UCDP counts that year, 772,463 were in Rwanda, almost all of them civilians killed deliberately in the genocide, in about a hundred days from April. UCDP's range for Rwanda that year is 347,614 to 1,612,663. It was one event in one country. The five-year average carries it from 1994 to 1998, so the plateau in the deaths line on the level over those years is a single event spread across five. The five years to 2025 contain no event of that size. On UCDP's best estimate each of them had more than 170,000 deaths, 2023 narrowly, and the heaviest wars changed: Ethiopia in 2021 and 2022, Ukraine from 2022, Gaza from 2023, Sudan from 2023, second only to Ukraine in 2025 and the site of most of that year's deliberate killings of civilians. That is what the average above the Rwanda years means, by a small margin and with the last two years still provisional: not a worse single year, but a level sustained for five.

    What the figures ask

    A high year is not a world war. Most years of the 1980s had more wars at the level of 1,000 deaths a year than 2025, and the 1950s had Korea. What the counts show for 2025 is breadth and persistence: more conflicts active than in any year since 1946; as many countries with more than 1,000 deaths as in any year since 1989, level with 2024; and a five-year average of deaths higher than any before it, narrowly above the Rwanda years, with the last two years provisional. Whether that is a level of violence that markets and governments have learned to tolerate, as a rate of unemployment is tolerated, is the question the rest of this site asks. The level shows the whole run of years these figures come from.
